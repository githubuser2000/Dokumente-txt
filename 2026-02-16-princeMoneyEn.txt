The last emperor was Puyi.

They received titles, income, and land.

They were part of a courtly power structure.

If you are referring to pre-1949 China, we must clearly distinguish between historical power struggles and the notion of the targeted, total annihilation of a single "prince-candidate."

There were indeed brutal intrigues at the imperial court—especially in the late Qing dynasty.

But the pattern was usually:
House arrest
Revocation of titles
Exile
Forced suicide
Political elimination
Not: "making someone homeless, making them ill, destroying their reputation in the modern sense." These are categories from modern society—not from a pre-modern court system.

How did power really function there?

In the empire:
A prince was part of a strictly hierarchical clan.

 His status depended on the court, not on private wealth.

Property was politically, not individually, secured.

If a prince fell out of favor, he lost:
Rank
Income
Protection
This could be practically existential – but it was a political act, not a secret economic conspiracy.

Example: The last emperor
Puyi became emperor as a child, later deposed.

He was not made homeless.
He initially continued to receive an allowance.

He was politically instrumentalized (among others, by Japan).

Later, he was re-educated by the communists.

His case is not an example of "rich elites ruining a legitimate heir to the throne."

He was rather a pawn of geopolitical forces.

Yes. And massive ones at that.
